401(k) Loan for Housing Deposits

Borrowing limits, repayment rules, job-loss risk, how mortgage lenders treat the loan, and whether Florida’s down-payment programs make it unnecessary — for both a traditional down payment and rent-to-own / lease-option deposits. You said your plan allows “a one time loan” — singular, possibly once ever. This brief reports that exactly as stated; whether any second loan is ever available after repayment is UNVERIFIED — nailing that down is checklist question #1.

Scope: IRS §72(p) mechanicsMortgage underwriting: FHA · Fannie Mae · Freddie MacFlorida: Hometown Heroes · Florida Assist

Bottom line

✓

Yes, a 401(k) loan can fund a down payment, closing costs, or a rent-to-own option fee. The IRS does not restrict what loan proceeds are used for — “the purpose of the loan … is irrelevant in determining whether the loan is permitted,” unlike hardship withdrawals. IRS FAQs on plan loans, read live

≤

“Wait until the balance is big enough” means 2× the deposit in vested balance under the standard limit. Max loan = lesser of 50% of vested balance or $50,000 — so a $10,000 deposit needs $20,000 vested. (The IRS also allows an optional $10,000 small-balance exception — up to $10,000 borrowable even with less than $20,000 vested — but plans are not required to offer it; verify with yours.) IRS FAQs, read live · IRS Retirement Topics: Loans

!

The dominant risk is job loss — but there is an escape route. Most plans accelerate repayment at separation; without action, an unpaid balance becomes a taxable distribution — income tax plus a 10% early-withdrawal penalty if under 59½. The escape route: if the balance becomes a qualified plan loan offset at separation, you have until the tax-filing deadline (including extensions) for the year of the offset to roll that amount into an IRA or another eligible plan, avoiding its treatment as a taxable distribution. Miss that deadline and the taxes plus the 10% penalty stand. IRS loan-offset rules

✓

Mortgage lenders treat it kindly: FHA, Fannie Mae, and Freddie Mac all allow the payment to be excluded from the debt-to-income ratio, because the loan is secured by your own asset. But the 401(k) balance counted as reserves must be reduced by the loan amount. Fannie B3-6-05 · HUD 4000.1 · Freddie LP guide

≠

Florida down-payment assistance usually makes the 401(k) loan unnecessary. Hometown Heroes (up to $35,000 at 0%, no monthly payment) or Florida Assist ($10,000 at 0%, deferred) can cover a full FHA 3.5% down payment — a no-payment second mortgage strictly dominates a 5-year amortizing loan. Combining the two is permissible in principle, but confirm with your participating lender. Florida Housing official page; lender summaries

Note

§1 · IRS mechanics

The IRS rules for 401(k) loans

How much you can borrow, how the 12-month lookback would limit repeat borrowing if your plan permits another loan at all, repayment terms, and who sets (and receives) the interest. IRS FAQs Regarding Loans — read live 2026-09-30

Maximum amount +

The plan maximum is (1) the greater of $10,000 or 50% of the vested account balance, or (2) $50,000 — whichever is less. A plan may set stricter limits, and a plan is not required to offer loans at all.

The $10,000 floor is optional: IRS guidance notes “plans are not required to include this exception.” IRS Retirement Topics: Loans

Only vested balance counts — unvested employer match or profit-sharing is excluded. Vesting schedules (cliff vs. graded) directly control when the balance becomes borrowable. Motley Fool 401(k) loan rules

The 12-month “highest outstanding balance” reduction +

Any new loan plus all outstanding plan loans cannot exceed the plan maximum. For the $50,000 prong, you subtract: (highest outstanding balance of all plan loans during the 12 months ending the day before the new loan) minus (outstanding balance on the date of the new loan). IRS FAQ Q8, worked example

IRS example: $80,000 vested; borrowed $27,000 eight months ago; still owes $18,000. Ceiling = lesser of [$50,000 − ($27,000 − $18,000) = $41,000] or [50% × $80,000 = $40,000] → $40,000 total; new loan max = $40,000 − $18,000 = $22,000.

Key nuance

Re-borrowing is conditional on your plan permitting any further loan at all: you said “a one time loan,” and whether a second loan is ever available after repayment is UNVERIFIED. Separately, paying off the old loan first barely helps — the IRS shows repaying the $18,000 would still cap the new loan at $23,000, because the $27,000 high-water mark counts for a full 12 months. So if your plan does allow another loan, after repayment you must still wait out the 12-month lookback to recover full capacity under the $50,000 prong (the 50%-of-vested prong is unaffected by history).

Repayment term & the primary-residence exception +

General rule: repay within 5 years, in “substantially equal payments that include principal and interest,” paid at least quarterly (most plans do payroll deduction each paycheck). “Loan repayments are not plan contributions.”

Primary-residence exception (IRC §72(p)(2)(B)(ii)): a loan taken to purchase the employee’s principal residence may be repaid over more than 5 years. In practice the plan decides whether to offer it and for how long — commonly up to 10–15 years (one example plan form offered 61–179 months). Example plan loan application, Cumberland Heights

Strict condition

The IRS requires the plan sponsor to obtain documentation of the home purchase before approving a longer term — self-certification is not enough. Leave of absence: repayments may be suspended up to 1 year, but the 5-year term is not extended — missed payments must be made up. IRS plan-sponsor guidance

Interest rate — who sets it, who gets it +

The plan administrator sets the rate; it must be “similar to what a participant might expect to receive from a financial institution.” Industry norm is prime rate + 1 to 2 percentage points. IRS fix-it guide · Motley Fool · accountinginsights

Who receives the interest: you — it is credited back to your own 401(k) account. But it is paid with after-tax dollars (payroll deduction), and the interest is taxed again on withdrawal in retirement.

Your “one time loan” — possibly once ever +

You said your plan allows “a one time loan” — singular. That may mean once ever, or it may merely mean one outstanding loan at a time with re-borrowing allowed after full repayment. Whether any second loan is ever available after repayment is UNVERIFIED: a one-loan limit is a plan design choice — the IRS itself permits multiple concurrent loans — and only your administrator can confirm what your plan means. If the loan is once ever, treat this as your single use and make the housing deposit count. ASPPA: 401(k) Loans — Considerations for a Plan

§2 · The dominant risk

Job loss / separation

What happens to the outstanding loan if you leave or lose the job. This is the single biggest tail risk of a 401(k) loan.

Core risk

No single IRS deadline — your plan sets the rule

Plan practices at separation vary: some require immediate lump-sum payoff; some re-amortize to monthly direct payments (check/ACH) to the recordkeeper; some allow repayment through the end of the cure period. The borrower must check their plan’s rule — the IRS does not mandate one. NAPA Net, Aug 2025

Default → “deemed distribution”

If the loan isn’t repaid per plan terms, it defaults and the entire outstanding balance becomes a deemed distribution: taxable as ordinary income that year, plus the 10% additional tax if under 59½ — with no exception for job loss.

A deemed distribution is not eligible for rollover. Mid-employment defaults get only a cure period to quarter-end — not the extended deadline. IRS FAQ Q5/Q6 · IRS cure-period issue snapshot

Offset → the narrow grace period

If instead the plan offsets the unpaid balance against the account, that’s treated as an actual distribution and is eligible for rollover.

For a qualified plan loan offset — loan in good standing, offset due to severance or plan termination, within 12 months after severance — the deadline is extended to your tax-filing due date, including extensions, for the year of the offset (Tax Cuts and Jobs Act change). Other offsets keep the standard 60-day rollover rule — check the Form 1099-R. IRS: Plan Loan Offsets · IRS FAQ Q7

Also

The offset shrinks your final payout

The outstanding balance is subtracted from the account — you receive (or roll over) only the net vested balance minus the loan — and you permanently lose the tax-advantaged growth on the offset amount. Practical takeaway: the grace period is real but narrow. Anyone borrowing for a house deposit should have a backup source (emergency fund, or funds to roll over) to cover the loan if employment ends.

§3 · Use of proceeds

Using the loan for housing

Down payment, closing costs, earnest money, rent-to-own option fees, security deposits — the money is yours to use freely once disbursed.

The rule

There are effectively no restrictions on use

“As long as a plan provides for loans, the purpose of the loan or the participant’s ability to borrow the same amount elsewhere is irrelevant in determining whether the loan is permitted, unlike hardship withdrawals.” A 401(k) loan is not a withdrawal: no 10% penalty and no immediate tax, as long as the amount/duration/repayment rules are met. IRS FAQs — read live

Two indirect constraints

1. The >5-year repayment term requires the plan to document an actual principal-residence purchase before approval — option fees and lease-option deposits don’t qualify for the extended term.

2. Some plans cap loans to employee contributions only, or restrict purposes by design — confirm with the administrator. ASPPA

Rent-to-own / lease-option deposits

Permitted as a use, on the standard term. The deposit/option fee itself is a perfectly allowable use of proceeds. But until the option is exercised and the purchase closes, the plan cannot document a “purchase” for the longer repayment term — so expect the 5-year rule.

Analysis grounded in the IRS documentation requirement above, not a separate IRS ruling on lease-options.

§4 · Mortgage-lender treatment

FHA & conventional underwriting

How FHA and conventional (Fannie Mae / Freddie Mac) underwriters treat an outstanding 401(k) loan: DTI, documentation, source of down payment, and reserves.

DTI treatment — the payment is generally EXCLUDED from monthly debt obligations
ProgramRuleDocumentation required
Fannie Mae Selling Guide B3-6-05, ed. 08/05/2026 — read live “Loans Secured by Financial Assets”: lender is not required to include the payment in recurring monthly debt obligations, provided the lender obtains the loan instrument showing the 401(k) as collateral. Copy of the loan instrument showing the financial asset as collateral.
FHA HUD Handbook 4000.1; mirror + corroborating lender write-ups “Loans secured against deposited funds, where repayment may be obtained through extinguishing the asset and these funds are not included in calculating the Borrower’s assets, do not require consideration of repayment for qualifying purposes.” Applies in both TOTAL (automated) and manual underwriting. Lender must verify/document the assets used to collateralize, the promissory note, and the loan proceeds; the corresponding asset must be reduced by the loan amount.
Freddie Mac Freddie LP correspondent underwriting guide (index) “Payments on installment debts secured by financial assets in which repayment may be obtained by liquidating the asset, may be excluded from the monthly debt payment-to-income ratio for qualifying purposes, regardless of the payment amount or number of payments remaining.” Underwriter documents monthly payment, asset value/ownership, and that the loan is secured by that asset; only assets exceeding the loan balance count as borrower funds.

Source of down payment

FHA treats a 401(k)-secured loan as a collateralized loan — an acceptable source of funds (including toward the borrower’s minimum required investment) with verification of collateral, note, and proceeds. Unsecured loans and credit-card advances are unacceptable. HUD 4000.1 asset section, via mirror

Fannie Mae: borrowed funds secured by an asset (explicitly including 401(k)s) are an acceptable source for down payment/closing costs with documentation of terms, transfer, and asset value/ownership — and the provider must not be a party to the transaction. Cardinal Financial conventional guide, ch. 9

Reserves shrink

Yes — borrowing reduces countable reserves. Fannie Mae: if the borrower intends to use the same asset to satisfy reserve requirements, the lender must reduce the asset’s value by the loan proceeds and any related fees. FHA: “The Mortgagee must reduce the amount of the corresponding asset by the amount of the collateralized loan.” Fannie B3-6-05 · HUD 4000.1

A $60,000 401(k) with a $10,000 loan counts as $50,000 (minus fees) toward reserves.

Two things to know

Not on the credit report · overlays may be stricter

401(k) loans do not appear on credit reports — defaults have no credit-score consequences. The lender learns of the loan from your application disclosures, bank statements, or the loan paperwork itself. And individual lenders may impose overlays stricter than agency minimums. Wharton PRC WP2014-01

§5 · Florida down-payment assistance

Hometown Heroes & Florida Assist

Florida’s assistance can cover the entire FHA 3.5% down payment — likely making a 401(k) loan unnecessary for an eligible buyer while funds last.

Hometown Heroes · 2026

Up to 5% of the first-mortgage amount (min $10,000, max $35,000) for down payment + closing costs — 0% interest, non-amortizing, 30-year deferred second mortgage. Not forgivable: due in full on sale, refinance, deed transfer, or loss of primary-residence occupancy.

Pairs with FHA, VA, USDA-RD, or conventional first mortgages at competitive rates, with reduced upfront fees and no origination/discount points. Eligibility: first-time, income-qualified buyers in eligible workforce occupations (health care, school staff, first responders, public safety/court, child care; military/veterans), full-time with a Florida-based employer, primary residence. Minimum 640 credit score; 2026 income limits at 150% of state/local median income.

2026 round: $50M opened 7/13/2026, first-come first-served; >$35M remained as of 8/24/2026. floridahousing.org — read live; WFLA; LinkedIn funding update 08-25-2026

Florida Assist

$10,000 at 0%, non-amortizing, no monthly payment — deferred second mortgage; repayable on sale/refinance/transfer/loss of occupancy.

Pairs with FHFC first mortgages (FHA/VA/USDA/HFA Preferred/HFA Advantage); 640 FICO; first-time buyer; homebuyer education; county income/purchase-price limits; one-time use across FHFC programs.

The amortizing sibling, FL HLP, is now $12,500 at 3% over 30 years ≈ $52.70/mo — that payment does count in DTI. FHFC board package 1/21/2022; 2026 program summaries

Comparison

DPA usually wins for the down payment itself

FHA requires 3.5% down: on a $285,700 purchase that’s $10,000 — exactly Florida Assist’s amount. HTH’s $10k–$35k band (avg. ~$16k on a ~$320k mortgage per 2026 lender data) covers the down payment plus a share of closing costs. A 0%-interest, no-payment second mortgage strictly dominates a 5-year amortizing 401(k) loan (prime+1–2%, payroll deduction, opportunity cost, job-loss risk) as a funding source.

Caveats: DPA funds are finite and first-come-first-served; the buyer still needs earnest money, reserves, and must meet credit/income/occupation rules; the DPA is repayable debt, not a grant.

Combination

Can a 401(k) loan be combined with DPA?

No Florida Housing source found prohibits a concurrent 401(k) loan. FHFC’s binding constraint is structural: the DPA must be paired with a Florida Housing first mortgage — the first mortgage’s agency rules then govern other borrowed funds. Under those rules, a 401(k)-secured loan is an acceptable, documented source of funds whose payment is excluded from DTI. So combining is permissible in principle — but check with the participating lender, since lender overlays and the DPA reservation process control the file.

No FHFC source explicitly addresses concurrent 401(k) loans — this is grounded inference, listed as an open question below.

§6 · Vs. saving cash separately

Pros and cons

Where the 401(k) loan beats the alternatives — and where it loses. Motley Fool · Porte Brown · Kiplinger · moneynav

Pros
  • No credit check or underwriting; fast access.
  • Interest (~prime+1%) paid to yourself vs. personal-loan rates of 6–36%.
  • No 10% early-withdrawal penalty and no immediate tax (unlike a withdrawal); money is repaid into the account.
  • Default doesn’t touch the credit score and triggers no collections.
Cons
  • Opportunity cost (the big one): borrowed money is out of the market for the loan term; the ~prime+1% “return” usually trails expected equity returns, and compounding losses snowball. One illustration: a $10,000 loan taken 20 years before retirement can forfeit $60,000+ of growth at assumed market returns.
  • Double taxation (nuanced): repayments are made with after-tax dollars, then taxed again on withdrawal. Strictly, the clearly double-taxed piece is the interest. Popular summaries describe the whole repayment as effectively taxed twice — directionally true for interest, overstated for principal, but a real (if modest) drag. Porte Brown · Kiplinger · Bogleheads forum
  • Contribution suspension: many plans bar new contributions while a loan is outstanding; even when allowed, the payroll deduction often crowds contributions out — forfeiting the employer match, the pre-tax deduction (raising current taxable income), and compounding. Kiplinger · moneynav
  • Fees: origination fees are the norm (most common: $50, then $75); some plans add quarterly maintenance fees; typically deducted from proceeds or the account. ASPPA
  • Job-loss acceleration: the single biggest tail risk — unpaid balance becomes taxable + 10% penalty under 59½.
  • Lost ERISA creditor protection on money outside the plan; non-deductible interest (unlike mortgage interest).

Scenarios where it beats the alternatives

Vs. outright withdrawal: almost always wins (no 10% penalty, money restored).

Vs. Roth IRA: withdrawing contributions (not earnings) from a Roth IRA is tax- and penalty-free and creates no debt, job-loss risk, or DTI paperwork — it usually beats a 401(k) loan for small deposit amounts, at the cost of permanently removing that Roth space.

Vs. waiting and saving: wins when the purchase is time-sensitive and the loan is short (bridge-like, repaid in 1–3 years) — e.g., locking a price/rate, avoiding rent, or avoiding PMI — provided contributions continue and the job is stable. The breakeven logic: the 401(k) loan wins if (consumer-loan interest saved) > (lost investment earnings) — but that arithmetic ignores taxes and job-loss risk.

Where it loses

Loses to waiting/saving when the timeline is flexible.

Loses to Florida DPA (0%, no payment) for eligible buyers.

Loses to any low-rate secured borrowing that doesn’t jeopardize retirement.

§7 · The verdict

When it’s reasonable — and when it’s not

Reasonable when ALL hold
  • The deposit need is modest relative to vested balance (≤ 50%, leaving a cushion), and the loan is repaid in 1–3 years — minimizing time out of the market.
  • The plan allows continued contributions + full employer match during repayment (verify in writing).
  • Employment is stable and there’s a backup repayment source (emergency fund) if separation occurs.
  • You’ve been denied or timed out of Florida DPA (Hometown Heroes / Florida Assist at 0% with no payment strictly dominates), and the alternative is high-rate debt or losing a time-sensitive purchase.
  • For rent-to-own: the option fee is small, the path to exercising the purchase is concrete, and you accept the 5-year repayment term.
Not reasonable when
  • The plan suspends contributions/match during repayment — the true cost then includes lost match + lost deduction + lost compounding.
  • The job or income is unstable — job-loss acceleration converts the loan into a taxable distribution + 10% penalty.
  • You’d need the IRS $10,000 floor exception and the plan doesn’t offer it, or vesting leaves you short (don’t borrow against a balance you can’t actually reach).
  • The timeline is flexible — saving cash separately avoids every risk above.
  • You’re eligible for Hometown Heroes or Florida Assist while funds remain — take the 0%, no-payment second mortgage instead.
  • It becomes a habit — if your plan ever permits re-borrowing at all (UNVERIFIED): serial 401(k) loans are the strongest predictor of retirement shortfalls (the 12-month lookback rule itself penalizes repeat borrowing).
§8 · Before you count on it

Questions for your plan administrator

Your specific plan’s terms are unknowable from the web — this checklist is the instrument to get them. Bring it to your administrator (or HR/your plan’s service center) before counting on the loan.

  1. Is this a once-ever loan, or one outstanding loan at a time with re-borrowing allowed after full repayment?

  2. Do you allow participant loans, and — if any further loan is ever permitted — is there a waiting period after payoff?

  3. What is the minimum loan amount, the maximum (any cap below IRS limits), and is the $10,000 IRS floor exception adopted?

  4. What are the origination and ongoing fees, and how are they charged?

  5. What is the current interest rate and its formula (prime + ?)? Is interest credited to my account?

  6. May I continue pre-tax contributions and receive the full employer match while repaying?

  7. Do you offer a >5-year principal-residence term, what is the maximum, and what purchase documentation do you require before approval? (Confirm: option fees/lease-option deposits get the standard 5-year term.)

  8. What happens at separation — immediate full payoff, continued direct payments, or a cure period? How long?

  9. Is spousal consent required? Which investments are sold to fund the loan, and how are repayments reinvested?

  10. How quickly are funds disbursed, and what loan documents will I receive (my mortgage lender will need the loan instrument)?
